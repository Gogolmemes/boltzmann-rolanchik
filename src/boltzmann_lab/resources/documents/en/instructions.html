User Guide

Welcome to the interactive particle motion simulation environment!

This program provides a visual way to explore physical processes involving particle motion and distribution, interactions with a potential energy field, and different types of barriers.

With this simulation, you can create your own energy landscapes, observe changes in the spatial distribution of particles, investigate statistical patterns, and conduct virtual experiments, including experiments involving Maxwell's demon.

The program combines visual simulation with statistical analysis tools, allowing you to compare numerical experiments with theoretical predictions from statistical physics.

1. Getting Started

1.1. Launching the Simulation

When you open the program, you will see the main menu. To enter the simulation workspace, click “Simulation”.

The simulation interface will open, displaying a visualization area, graphs of physical quantities, and a panel for controlling system parameters.

Please note that particles do not start moving immediately after you enter the workspace. You must first define the system's potential energy.

Figure 1. Program Main Menu

1.2. Setting the Potential Energy

The first step is to define the potential energy function U(x), which describes how a particle's potential energy depends on its horizontal position.

Potential energy influences particle motion and the formation of their spatial distribution. In particular, at thermal equilibrium, regions of lower potential energy are more favorable for particles, all other conditions being equal.

The program provides two ways to define the potential energy.

Method 1. Using a Preset Graph

  1. Find the “Preset Graphs” section in the control panel.
  2. Select one of the available potential energy profiles.
  3. Click “Apply Preset”.
  4. The program will apply the selected U(x) function and automatically start the simulation.

This method is recommended for first-time users and for experiments with predefined energy landscapes.

Figure 2. Selecting a Preset Potential Energy Profile

Method 2. Drawing a Custom Graph

If you want to define the conditions of particle motion yourself, use the potential energy drawing tool.

  1. Click “Draw New Graph”.
  2. Move the mouse pointer into the upper graph area.
  3. Hold down the left mouse button and draw a line from left to right to define the desired shape of U(x).
  4. When you finish drawing, click “Done / Apply”.
  5. The program will apply the custom graph and automatically start the simulation.

When drawing a graph, you can create regions with different potential energy values, forming energy minima, maxima, and transition regions.

For example, you can create a potential well—a region of reduced potential energy where particles tend to accumulate at sufficiently low temperatures.

Another option is a potential barrier, a region of increased energy that makes it more difficult for particles to move between different parts of the system.

Figure 3. Creating a Custom Graph

Important! The simulation will not start until potential energy has been defined. To start particle motion, apply a preset graph or finish creating your own.

1.3. Controlling Playback

After you define the potential energy, the simulation starts automatically.

Use the “Pause / Start” button to control playback.

  • Pause temporarily stops the simulation so you can examine the current particle positions and statistical values.
  • Start resumes the simulation after a pause.

Pausing is especially useful in experiments where you need to capture and analyze a particular state of the system.

2. Program Interface

The workspace consists of several functional areas. Each displays particular physical characteristics or provides controls for the simulation.

Figure 4. Simulation Interface Overview

2.1. Upper Graph — Potential Energy U(x)

The upper part of the visualization area displays the potential energy graph U(x).

The horizontal coordinate x indicates the particle's position in the system, while U(x) describes its potential energy at that position.

The shape of the graph determines the energy landscape in which particles move.

The main features of this landscape are:

  • Potential well — a region where potential energy is lower than in neighboring areas.
  • Potential barrier — a region of increased potential energy that requires sufficient mechanical energy to cross.
  • Horizontal section — a region of constant potential energy where no force arises from the horizontal potential gradient.
  • Sloped section — a region where potential energy changes with position and a force acts on the particle according to the derivative of potential energy.

In one dimension, force and potential energy are related by:

F(x) = −dU(x)/dx

Thus, the force points in the direction of decreasing potential energy.

Figure 5. Example of a Potential Barrier

2.2. Particle Field — Motion Visualization

The central part of the interface is the field in which particles move.

Each particle is displayed as a colored circle. Its position changes during the simulation according to the rules of motion and interaction implemented in the program.

Speed Color Coding

A particle's color provides an approximate indication of its speed.

  • Light blue shades indicate relatively slow particles.
  • Intermediate shades indicate particles moving at moderate speeds.
  • Yellow and red shades indicate the fastest particles.

This color scale lets you observe differences in particle speeds and changes in their kinetic states.

Particle Field Background

The background color represents the spatial distribution of potential energy U(x).

This allows you to compare particle positions with the energy landscape and observe how potential energy affects their spatial distribution.

Center-of-Mass Line

The yellow vertical line indicates the horizontal position of the particle system's center of mass.

If all particles have equal masses, the center-of-mass position is the arithmetic mean of their horizontal coordinates:

xcm = (1/N) Σ xi

Here, N is the total number of particles, and xi is the horizontal coordinate of the i-th particle.

Changes in this line's position reveal shifts in the system's center of mass, for example when particles accumulate in a particular region.

Figure 6. Particle Motion Field

2.3. Lower Graph — Particle Distribution

The lower graph is intended for statistical analysis of the particles' spatial distribution.

It displays both the experimental distribution obtained from the simulation and a theoretical curve based on the Boltzmann distribution.

Experimental Histogram

The histogram shows how particles are distributed along the horizontal coordinate.

To construct it, the space is divided into intervals. The number of particles in each interval is used to form the experimental distribution.

As particles move, the histogram changes to reflect the current state of the system.

Theoretical Boltzmann Curve

The theoretical curve describes the expected equilibrium distribution of particles in a potential field.

In the arbitrary units used by the program, the relationship is:

p(x) ∝ exp(−U(x)/T)

where:

  • p(x) is the probability density of finding a particle near position x;
  • U(x) is the particle's potential energy at that position;
  • T is the system's temperature parameter;
  • exp is the exponential function.

The normalized distribution can be written as:

p(x) = exp(−U(x)/T) / Z

where Z is the normalization factor:

Z = ∫ exp(−U(x)/T) dx

Integration is performed over the range of horizontal coordinates accessible to particles.

The formula shows that regions of lower potential energy have a higher equilibrium probability of containing a particle, all other conditions being equal.

As temperature increases, differences in potential energy have a weaker effect on the distribution.

Conversely, as temperature decreases, particles become more concentrated in energetically favorable regions.

Note. The theoretical Boltzmann curve describes an equilibrium distribution. During transient processes, in the presence of impermeable barriers, or when special particle-sorting rules are active, the experimental histogram may differ significantly from this curve.

Figure 7. Comparing Experimental and Theoretical Distributions

2.4. Statistics Bar

The statistics bar displays the main quantitative characteristics of the system.

It lets you monitor changes in physical quantities directly during the simulation.

Center of Mass

Displays the current horizontal coordinate of the particles' center of mass.

This value allows you to quantify shifts in the particle distribution relative to its initial state.

Entropy

Entropy is used to characterize the spatial distribution of particles.

The program uses the expression:

S = −Σ pi ln(pi)

where pi is the fraction of particles in the i-th spatial interval.

The sum is taken over all histogram intervals. An empty interval contributes zero.

This quantity represents the information entropy of the discrete spatial distribution.

For a fixed number of equal-width intervals, a more uniform particle distribution generally corresponds to higher entropy, while a strong concentration of particles in a small region corresponds to lower entropy.

Note that this quantity characterizes the spatial distribution only; it is not the system's complete thermodynamic entropy.

Temperature

Temperature characterizes the kinetic state of particles and is related to their average kinetic energy.

During the simulation, its value helps you assess changes in the intensity of particle motion.

Particle Counts on Either Side of the Barrier

When a barrier is present, the program displays the number of particles to its left and right.

These values are particularly useful for studying barrier permeability, directional particle transport, and Maxwell's demon.

3. Configuring Simulation Parameters

The control panel lets you change the system's main parameters and investigate how different conditions affect particle motion and distribution.

Figure 8. Simulation Parameters Panel

3.1. Number of Particles

The “Number of Particles” parameter determines how many particles participate in the simulation.

Increasing the number of particles lets you observe a larger system and obtain more statistically stable distributions.

With a small number of particles, random fluctuations in the histogram can be particularly noticeable.

With more particles, the experimental distribution generally becomes smoother at equilibrium and reproduces the expected statistical patterns more closely.

However, increasing the number of particles also increases the computational load.

3.2. Particle Radius

The “Radius” parameter determines the geometric size of particles.

It affects their visual appearance and their ability to pass through narrow openings and between obstacles.

In particular, when using a porous barrier, a particle can pass through an opening only if its size allows it to fit.

Changing the radius therefore lets you investigate how the permeability of geometric barriers depends on particle size.

3.3. Temperature T

Temperature is one of the key simulation parameters.

It is related to the particles' average kinetic energy and determines the nature of their thermal motion.

As temperature increases, particles move faster on average, and the effect of potential barriers on their spatial distribution becomes less pronounced.

As temperature decreases, average speeds decrease and the relative influence of potential energy increases.

At low temperatures, particles may become concentrated primarily in potential wells.

Temperature also appears in the Boltzmann equilibrium distribution, so changing it directly affects the shape of the theoretical curve.

3.4. Energy Scale

The “Energy Scale” parameter lets you change the relative magnitude of potential energy.

It determines how strongly the chosen energy landscape affects particles compared with their characteristic thermal energy.

Increasing the energy scale:

  • Makes potential wells more energetically pronounced.
  • Strengthens the effect of potential barriers on particle motion.
  • May make the equilibrium distribution less uniform.
  • May increase particle concentration in regions of low potential energy.

Decreasing the energy scale weakens the effect of the potential field and, all other conditions being equal, makes the equilibrium distribution more uniform.

The ratio of the characteristic potential energy to temperature is especially important for understanding system behavior.

Thus, changing the energy scale and changing the temperature can have similar effects on the equilibrium distribution, although these operations have different physical meanings.

3.5. Redistributing Particles

The “Redistribute” button repositions particles according to the Boltzmann distribution.

When this function is used, particle positions are generated based on the specified potential energy function and temperature parameter.

This allows you to obtain a spatial distribution corresponding to the selected theoretical relationship immediately, without waiting for it to develop through particle motion.

The feature is particularly useful for comparing different energy landscapes and temperature conditions.

4. Working with Barriers

The program allows you to place different types of barriers and obstacles in the particle field.

They are intended for investigating particle reflection, restrictions on movement, geometric permeability, and directional transport.

4.1. Selecting a Barrier Type

To configure an obstacle, find the “Barrier” group in the control panel.

Here, you can select the required barrier type and adjust its parameters.

The available options differ in geometry and in their rules of interaction with particles.

4.2. Changing the Barrier Position

You can change the barrier position in two ways.

Method 1. Numerical Input

Enter the desired coordinate in the corresponding numerical input field in the control panel.

Method 2. Dragging with the Mouse

  1. Move the mouse pointer over the red barrier line in the particle field.
  2. Make sure the cursor changes to a horizontal double-headed arrow ↔.
  3. Press the left mouse button.
  4. Hold the button and drag the barrier to the desired position.
  5. Release the mouse button to set the new position.

Dragging allows you to change the geometry of the space accessible to particles while the model is running.

Figure 9. Moving the Barrier

4.3. Solid Barrier

A solid barrier is an impermeable wall that completely divides the available space.

Particles cannot pass freely through it and are reflected when they collide with it.

This obstacle is suitable for investigating particle motion in confined regions, changes in distribution caused by a moving wall, and mechanical interactions between a barrier and the particle system.

4.4. Porous Barrier

A porous barrier is a wall with three openings through which particles may pass.

Whether a particle can pass depends on the geometric dimensions of the particle and the opening.

If the particle fits through a pore, it can move to the other side of the barrier.

If its size prevents it from passing through, it interacts with the obstacle.

This mode lets you investigate how particle size affects the permeability of geometric barriers.

4.5. Scatterer Barrier

In “Scatterers” mode, arrays of geometric obstacles are used instead of a continuous wall.

Depending on the selected configuration, the obstacles may be circular, elliptical, or triangular.

Particles change their direction of motion when interacting with these objects.

This allows you to observe complex trajectories and investigate how obstacle geometry affects particle transport.

Available Geometric Configurations

  • Circles — circular obstacles.
  • Ellipses — elongated obstacles.
  • Triangles — obstacles with straight edges and angular vertices.

Gradient Scatterer Systems

Additional special configurations are available:

  • “Gradient →” — a system of five rows of circles with radii that change progressively from left to right.
  • “Gradient ←” — a system of five rows of circles with the opposite direction of radius variation.

In these modes, obstacle geometry varies along the horizontal coordinate.

This lets you investigate how spatial variations in obstacle geometry affect particle motion and distribution.

Figure 10. Examples of Scatterer Types

4.6. Semipermeable Barrier

A semipermeable barrier uses a probabilistic mechanism to determine whether particles can pass through it.

Each time a particle collides with the barrier, the program randomly determines whether it can cross.

The transmission probability is specified by the user.

Two parameters can be configured independently:

  • Left-to-right transmission probability — the probability that a particle crosses from the left side of the system to the right.
  • Right-to-left transmission probability — the probability that a particle crosses from the right side to the left.

If the transmission probability is zero, passage in that direction is prohibited.

If the probability is one, passage in that direction is always allowed.

At intermediate values, the decision is made randomly at each collision.

Different transmission probabilities in opposite directions allow you to simulate directional particle transport.

For example, a higher left-to-right probability than right-to-left probability may cause particles to accumulate preferentially on the right.

4.7. Work Performed by a Moving Barrier

When a barrier moves, interactions with particles may change their velocities.

In the program, a moving barrier is treated as an infinitely massive wall.

This means that collisions with particles do not change the barrier's motion, but the barrier's velocity can affect the velocities of reflected particles.

The “Barrier Performs Work” checkbox controls this effect.

Checkbox Enabled

The barrier's motion is taken into account during reflection.

As a result, a particle may gain or lose kinetic energy depending on the direction of wall motion and the collision conditions.

For example, a wall moving toward a particle may increase its speed after reflection.

Checkbox Disabled

Reflection occurs as though the barrier were stationary.

Thus, the wall's geometric position may change, but the additional velocity change caused by wall motion is not taken into account.

Physical interpretation. For an ideal elastic reflection from an infinitely massive wall moving horizontally at velocity V, the particle's horizontal velocity after the collision is vx, after = 2V − vx, before. For a stationary wall, V = 0, so the horizontal velocity component simply reverses sign.

5. Maxwell's Demon Mode

5.1. Purpose of the Mode

“Maxwell's Demon” mode is designed for experiments involving controlled particle transmission and reflection.

It is based on the well-known thought experiment in statistical physics proposed by James Clerk Maxwell.

In the classical thought experiment, a demon can observe individual molecules and selectively open or close a passage between two compartments.

In this way, the demon can sort molecules by a particular property, such as speed.

In this program, users can control particle transmission manually or configure automatic sorting rules based on color categories.

The mode lets you investigate how selective control of particle movement affects their distribution between the two parts of the system.

5.2. Activating the Mode

  1. Find the “Mode” drop-down menu at the top of the left control panel.
  2. Open the list of available modes.
  3. Select “Maxwell's Demon”.

The program switches to a special mode for controlling particle interactions with the barrier.

The barrier becomes a stationary line, and particles are displayed using one, two, or three color categories.

These categories can be used to configure automatic transmission and reflection rules.

Figure 11. Maxwell's Demon Mode Interface

5.3. Manual Particle Control

In Maxwell's Demon mode, users can decide individually whether particles should pass through the barrier.

When a particle reaches the barrier and its subsequent motion is not determined by an automatic rule, the simulation pauses.

You must then choose one of two actions.

Action 1. Allow the Particle to Pass

To allow a particle to cross the barrier:

  • Click the “Allow” button in the interface.
  • Alternatively, press the → arrow key.
  • Or press the Spacebar.

The particle will then be allowed to continue through the barrier.

Action 2. Reflect the Particle

To prevent the particle from crossing:

  • Click the “Reflect” button.
  • Alternatively, press the ← arrow key.
  • Or press Esc.

The particle is reflected and remains on its original side of the barrier.

Using these actions, you can manually sort particles according to a chosen property.

Figure 12. Making a Decision in Maxwell's Demon Mode

5.4. Automatic Rules

To reduce the need for manual actions, the program lets you create automatic particle transmission rules.

Rules are configured separately for each color category.

The following options are available for a selected color:

“Do Not Allow”

Particles in this color category are not automatically granted permission to cross the barrier.

Their subsequent behavior is determined by the “Otherwise” setting.

“Allow →”

Particles of the selected color are automatically allowed to cross the barrier from left to right.

When they attempt to cross in the opposite direction, the action specified under “Otherwise” is applied.

“Allow ←”

Particles of the selected color are automatically allowed to cross from right to left.

When they attempt to cross from left to right, the action specified under “Otherwise” is applied.

“Always Allow”

Particles of the selected color category are automatically allowed to cross the barrier in both directions.

This option is useful when a particular category should move freely between the two compartments.

5.5. Configuring the “Otherwise” Action

The “Otherwise” setting determines what happens when the selected automatic rule does not permit a particle to cross.

Two options are available.

“Ask”

The simulation pauses, and the user chooses whether to allow the particle to pass or reflect it.

This option combines automatic control with manual decision-making.

“Reflect”

Particles that are not permitted to pass under the automatic rule are reflected without user intervention.

This makes fully automatic particle sorting possible.

Figure 13. Configuring Automatic Rules

5.6. Example of Automatic Sorting

Consider an experiment intended to make particles of different colors accumulate on opposite sides of the barrier.

Suppose the system uses two color categories.

For the first category, select “Allow →”; for the second, select “Allow ←”.

Then select “Otherwise: Reflect” for both categories.

As a result:

  • Particles of the first category can cross from left to right but cannot return.
  • Particles of the second category can cross from right to left but cannot return to the right side.

As the simulation progresses, particles of the first category may accumulate on the right, while particles of the second category accumulate on the left.

The process runs automatically and does not require a decision at every collision.

You can assess the results by observing the spatial distribution and changes in the statistical indicators.

Figure 14. Automatic Particle Sorting

6. Recommendations for Conducting Experiments

For clear, interpretable results, follow several basic guidelines.

6.1. Change Parameters One at a Time

When investigating the effect of a particular parameter, try to keep all other settings unchanged.

For example, if you are studying how temperature affects particle distribution, avoid changing the shape of the potential energy function and the energy scale at the same time.

This will help you identify the cause of the observed changes more accurately.

6.2. Account for Statistical Fluctuations

Individual particle motion and particle distributions may exhibit noticeable fluctuations.

These fluctuations are particularly pronounced in systems containing relatively few particles.

Therefore, a single instantaneous state does not always represent the system's typical behavior.

For a more reliable assessment, observe the distribution over time or repeat the experiment.

6.3. Compare Experiment with Theory

The lower graph allows you to compare simulation results with the theoretical Boltzmann distribution.

When analyzing the results, consider whether the system is in equilibrium and whether additional restrictions prevent it from reaching the corresponding distribution.

6.4. Use Pause for Analysis

To examine a particular moment in detail, use the “Pause / Start” button.

This is especially useful when observing particle interactions with barriers and comparing statistical indicators.

6.5. Record Your Results

When conducting a series of experiments, it is useful to record the main system parameters:

  • Number of particles.
  • Particle radius.
  • Temperature.
  • Potential energy scale.
  • Barrier type and position.
  • Particle transmission rules, if applicable.

You can also save screenshots of the particle field, histogram, and statistics bar.

This makes it easier to compare results and present your experiments as small research projects.

7. Troubleshooting

7.1. The Simulation Does Not Start

Possible cause: No potential energy has been defined.

Solution:

Select a preset graph and click “Apply Preset”, or create your own function and click “Done / Apply”.

The simulation should start automatically once the potential energy has been applied.

7.2. Particles Have Stopped Moving

Possible cause: The simulation is paused.

Solution:

Check the “Pause / Start” button and resume the simulation if necessary.

If Maxwell's Demon mode is active, make sure the program is not waiting for a decision to allow or reflect a particle.

7.3. Keyboard Controls Do Not Work

Possible cause: Keyboard focus is in a numerical input field or another interface element.

Solution:

  1. Click an empty area of the particle field.
  2. Press the desired key again.
  3. If the problem persists, use the corresponding on-screen buttons.

When a numerical input field is active, arrow keys may be interpreted as commands to change that field's value.

7.4. The Experimental Distribution Does Not Match the Theoretical Curve

Such a discrepancy does not necessarily indicate an error.

Possible causes include:

  • The system has not yet reached equilibrium.
  • There are too few particles to produce a smooth histogram.
  • A barrier restricts the free movement of particles.
  • Special transmission or reflection rules are active.
  • Temporary statistical fluctuations are present.

To investigate, observe the system for longer, increase the number of particles, or redistribute the particles according to the Boltzmann distribution.

8. Returning to the Main Menu

To leave the simulation workspace, click “Main Menu”.

The current simulation will close, and its settings will be reset.

Warning! Before returning to the main menu, make sure you no longer need the current simulation parameters or results. The system state is not saved when you exit, so you will need to configure the simulation again for a new experiment.

9. Conclusion

The interactive particle motion simulation provides a visual way to explore a wide range of phenomena in statistical physics.

Creating custom potential energy landscapes, controlling temperature and system geometry, using different types of barriers, and conducting experiments with Maxwell's demon allow you to study physical principles not only theoretically but also through direct observation of a model.

One of the program's most valuable features is the ability to analyze both the motion of individual particles and the statistical characteristics of the entire system.

Comparing experimental distributions with theoretical relationships helps explain how the behavior of many particles gives rise to macroscopic physical laws.

The program can be used for independent study of statistical physics, educational experiments, demonstrations, and small research projects.
